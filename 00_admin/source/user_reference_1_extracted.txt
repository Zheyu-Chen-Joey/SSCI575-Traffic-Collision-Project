Time Allocation

Part 1 — Research Background and Significance
Target: approximately 3 minutes
Slide 1 — Project Introduction  |  ≈ 20 sec
Good morning. Our project is titled “Spatial Prediction of Traffic Collision Risk in Los Angeles.” We are interested in a simple but important spatial question: why are recorded traffic collisions concentrated in some parts of Los Angeles more than others, and how much of that spatial variation can be explained by the surrounding urban environment?

Slide 2 — Research Background  |  ≈ 40 sec
Traffic collisions are not distributed evenly across the city. A commercial corridor, a residential neighborhood, and a freeway environment have very different traffic exposure, road structure, population activity, and land use. Because these conditions vary from place to place, collision burden is naturally a spatial data science problem. Our goal is therefore not only to count collisions, but to connect their locations with the urban conditions surrounding them.

Slide 3 — Research Significance and Historical Trend  |  ≈ 1 min 20 sec
The historical trend shows why this question matters. According to LADOT’s 2024 Vision Zero Safety Study, reported traffic fatalities increased from 246 in 2017 to 294 in 2021, an increase of about 20 percent. Over the same period, fatal-and-severe-injury, or KSI, collisions increased from 1,472 to 1,658, about 13 percent.
The problem also continued after our study period. LADOT reported 336 traffic fatalities in 2023, which was a record level in recent years. In 2024, the number declined to 304, the first decline in several years, but this still represents hundreds of preventable deaths in one year.
The spatial concentration is equally important. The 2024 All Modes High Injury Network contains only 7.5 percent of city roadway mileage, but it includes 61.8 percent of fatal and severe-injury collisions from 2017 to 2021. So a relatively small share of streets contains a very large share of the most serious collision burden.
One caution is that LADOT also reports a major change in LAPD reporting practices in 2021 for lower-severity collisions. Therefore, a drop in recorded injury collisions does not automatically mean a true safety improvement. In our project, we describe the outcome carefully as recorded collision burden.

Slide 4 — Study Scope and Research Question  |  ≈ 40 sec
For our analysis, the study area is the City of Los Angeles and the period is 2017 through 2021. We divide the city into one-kilometer by one-kilometer grids. For each grid, our outcome is the total number of recorded collisions during the five-year period.
Our main research question is: how well can urban conditions predict spatial differences in recorded collision counts? The practical value is to identify areas that deserve closer safety investigation and to understand which urban conditions contain useful predictive information. This is a screening and prioritization tool, not a substitute for detailed engineering analysis.


Part 2 + Part 3 — Datasets and Research Method
Target: approximately 3 minutes total
Slide 5 — Proposed Datasets  |  ≈ 40 sec
Our project combines five public data sources. First, LAPD collision point records provide the target that we want to model. Second, LADOT traffic-count locations represent traffic exposure. Third, Census TIGER/Line roads provide the road network. Fourth, the 2021 American Community Survey provides block-group population data. Fifth, Los Angeles General Plan land-use polygons describe the surrounding land-use context.
The 2024 High Injury Network is kept separate. We use it only as a contextual comparison layer and do not include it as a model predictor.

Slide 6 — Six Predictors in One Grid Table  |  ≈ 30 sec
After spatial processing, each one-kilometer grid becomes one row in our model table. We use six predictor concepts: traffic volume, road density, intersection density, road type, population density, and land use. Continuous variables are summarized within each grid, while categorical variables such as road type and land use will be encoded before modeling. The target remains the five-year recorded collision count.

Slide 7 — Random Forest Regression  |  ≈ 50 sec
Our machine-learning method is Random Forest regression. A Random Forest is an ensemble of many decision trees. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the training observations, and random subsets of predictors are considered when the tree makes splits. Because the individual trees are different, their errors are less likely to be identical. For a grid, the forest averages the predictions from all trees to produce one final collision-count estimate.
We choose regression because our target is numerical. The final estimate can be fractional because it represents an average predicted count.

Slide 8 — Why Random Forest and How We Use It  |  ≈ 40 sec
Random Forest is useful for this project for three reasons. First, traffic-safety relationships may be nonlinear. Second, variables can interact: for example, high traffic volume may have different implications in a dense commercial area than in a residential area. Third, variable-importance measures can help us identify which inputs the model relies on most.
In our project, the six urban predictors are the inputs and the predicted five-year collision count per grid is the output. This is retrospective spatial prediction for 2017 to 2021. It is not a forecast of next year’s crashes, and variable importance does not prove causation.

Slide 9 — Planned Evaluation  |  ≈ 40 sec
For evaluation, we will compare held-out predictions with observed collision counts using R-squared, RMSE, and MAE. We will also inspect residual maps to see where the model systematically underpredicts or overpredicts.
Because nearby grids can be similar, we also plan to test sensitivity to spatial train-test separation. Finally, we will compare high-count grids with the official High Injury Network as contextual evidence. The HIN is not independent ground truth, so the main validation remains the held-out observed collision counts.


Part 4 — Previous Work and Presentation Summary
Target: approximately 3 minutes
Slide 10 — Related Study: Bao et al. (2017)  |  ≈ 1 min 15 sec
Our main related study is Bao and colleagues, published in Accident Analysis & Prevention in 2017. We selected this paper because it also studies traffic crashes in the City of Los Angeles and combines several types of spatial urban data within a common geographic unit.
The authors asked whether Twitter-based human-activity information could improve spatial crash analysis. They collected five broad types of data: crash data, human-activity data, traditional traffic-exposure measures, road-network attributes, and sociodemographic information. These data were aggregated into 896 traffic analysis zones, and the Twitter check-ins were classified into seven activity categories using venue information.
For modeling, the authors used Geographically Weighted Regression, or GWR. They compared models using traditional traffic-exposure variables only, Twitter-based activity variables only, and both types together. Their results showed that human-activity variables significantly affected crash counts at the zone level, and the combined models achieved the best fit, with higher R-squared and lower AICc values.

Slide 11 — How the Study Informs Our Design  |  ≈ 55 sec
The important lesson for us is not to copy their exact variables or model. Instead, the paper supports two design ideas: urban crash patterns should be analyzed together with the surrounding human and built environment, and data from different sources need a common spatial unit.
Our project adapts that idea in three ways. First, we use regular one-kilometer grids instead of 896 traffic analysis zones. Second, instead of Twitter activity, we use six predictors from public transportation, road, census, and land-use datasets. Third, we use one Random Forest regressor instead of GWR. Therefore, the earlier paper supports our design logic, but its coefficients and model performance cannot be transferred directly to our project.

Slide 12 — Project Summary and Closing  |  ≈ 50 sec
To conclude, our project combines a meaningful public-safety problem, multiple spatial datasets, and one manageable machine-learning model. We will create a grid-based view of recorded collision burden, test whether six urban conditions can predict that burden, and inspect where the model succeeds or fails spatially.
Before modeling, we still need to audit data coverage and timing, especially the 2021 reporting change and the uneven dates of traffic counts. Our next steps are to build the grid table, train and evaluate the Random Forest, map the residuals, and compare our high-count areas with the official HIN.
Thank you. We welcome feedback on whether our study scope, data choices, and proposed method are appropriate.
